Climbing is about as technically complex as sport gets: four limbs, each with countless positions relative to the others, meeting different holds, grip types, wall angles and rock types — before weather, conditions and fear get a say. Learning to move well as an adult is daunting, and the advice doesn't help much, because it contradicts itself. At one end, "just climb more"; at the other, coaches prescribing drills on and off the wall. How much climbing, of what kind, and which drills are worth the time? And more fundamentally, how would you know a change is working? The research on skilled movement acquisition answers that last question with a distinction. *Performance* is doing better in today's session — your coach hands you the beta and you send. *Learning* is what's still there sessions later (tested by retention) and what carries over to new situations, like a different wall angle (tested by transfer). What maximises performance today is often not what maximises learning. Studies on climbing technique itself are rare, so the series steps back to principles that seem to hold across skills generally, one episode each: specificity, variability, feedback, spacing, attention, imitation (observational learning), and control, agency and autonomy. Each episode covers the theory, the major studies for and against, and the practical applications. The caveats up front: my own research is basic systems neuroscience, not human motor learning; the field has its share of findings that don't replicate; much of it comes from simple lab tasks with novice adults, and principles from simple tasks don't always survive the move to complex skills like climbing.

The performance-versus-learning distinction is the formal version of what [[read-this-book-climbing-technique|John Kettle's book]] gets at about how you watch yourself climb, and it sharpens the self-supervised framework in [[identify-your-climbing-weaknesses|the weaknesses video]]: an improvement that appears in-session is a prediction to test, not a result. For why strength training doesn't compete with any of this, see [[role-of-strength-training-in-climbing|the role of strength training]].
