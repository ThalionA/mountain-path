The follow-up to [[how-strong-is-strong-enough|the benchmarks video]]: that one was about testing your strength, this one is about what's actually holding your climbing back — which might not be strength at all. The first move is a filter. A weakness only matters insofar as it limits the goal you actually have; borrowing the rate-limiting step from chemistry, speeding up every other step in the chain changes nothing until you find the slow one. The rest is three frameworks rather than a list of weaknesses to check yourself against, in ascending order of effort. Supervised: ask someone who already holds the model of what correct looks like — a coach, or peers who are less emotionally attached to your climbing than you are. Unsupervised: find the structure in your own data — logbook patterns, the shape of your pyramid, flash percentages by grade, where you sit relative to the published benchmark distributions (an outlier in either direction is informative). Self-supervised: commit to a prediction — read the sequence, say it out loud — then attend to where the prediction and the outcome disagree, and use video to check your imagined movement against the cold, hard reality of it. Surprise is the teaching signal. Two caveats close it out: the process is uncomfortable by construction, and one bad session is overfitting, not a diagnosis.

Sits downstream of [[dave-macleod-9-out-of-10-mistakes|Dave MacLeod's point]] that climbers plateau by polishing details instead of finding the big limiter, and next to [[role-of-strength-training-in-climbing|the case for strength training]] for what to do once you've found one. The next question — telling a surface weakness apart from its underlying cause, which in machine learning is called credit assignment — is its own video.
