This is the schedule behind [[brachialis-tendon-recovery|my brachialis tendon recovery]] — the progression that took me from not being able to lift a glass of water to one-arm pullups. The skeleton (banded reverse curls twice a day, then dumbbells, then pullups, then weighted pullups, and crucially *not* staying away from the wall) came from a physiotherapist, Phil Mack, after a proper assessment. The specific sets, reps, and progression criteria below are sensible defaults from standard tendon-loading practice, filled in so the schedule is actually usable — treat pain as the signal and adjust around it.

> [!warning] Not medical advice
> Deep upper-arm pain has more than one possible cause. This worked for a diagnosed brachialis tendon injury, *after* assessment by a physio. If your pain is sharp or hot, or it isn't trending better after two to three weeks of Phase 1, get assessed by someone who can actually examine your arm.

## Ground rules (apply to every phase)

- **Early loading, not complete rest.** The operating framework is POLICE (Protection, Optimal Loading, Ice, Compression, Elevation), which replaced the old RICE advice. Complete rest lets the pain settle and then every comeback re-injures you — I ran that two-weeks-off cycle for a year and a half and it went nowhere.
- **Pain traffic light.** Up to ~3/10 during or after a session, settling by the next morning: fine, carry on (green). 4–5/10: repeat the current load, don't progress (amber). Above that, or worse the next morning: drop back a step (red).
- **Patience and consistency.** The week numbers below are indicative. Tendons adapt on a timescale of months.

## Phase 1 — Banded (roughly weeks 1–4)

The single exercise from the video: **banded reverse curls**. Stand on a light resistance band, elbow pinned at your side, palm facing *down* — the pronated grip is what biases the brachialis over the biceps — and curl slowly: about three seconds up, brief pause, three seconds down.

- **2–3 sets of 15–20 reps, twice a day**, every day.
- **Plus one set before every climbing session**, as part of the warmup. This stays in the warmup permanently and eventually gets replaced by the dumbbell reverse curls below.
- Band choice: light enough that the last few reps are work but pain stays green. This should feel almost insultingly easy at first.

**Move to Phase 2 when:** two weeks of both daily sessions at green, through the full range of motion.

## Phase 2 — Weighted (roughly weeks 4–10)

Swap one of the daily band sessions for dumbbell work; keep the other band session and the pre-climb set.

- **Dumbbell reverse curls: 3 sets of 8–12**, slow on the way down (3–4 seconds), 2–3 sessions a week with a rest day between them.
- Add **hammer curls** as a second movement once reverse curls are comfortable if you'd like. I do them occasionally, but they're not really part of my current routine. 
- Load progression: smallest increment available, only after two consecutive sessions fully at green.
- I still use only 5kg for these curls during my warmup (1 set of 8 reps per arm, before my first pullups of the session)

**Move to Phase 3 when:** reverse curls at a meaningful load are painless and moderate-grade climbing provokes nothing.

## Phase 3 — Pulling (weeks 10 onwards)

- **Pullups** — bodyweight, controlled, full range — then **weighted pullups** in small jumps, same two-green-sessions rule before adding load.
- As maintenance, I keep trying to do one set of weighted dumbbell curls per day forever. 
- The long game: this same ladder eventually carried on to [[one-arm-pullup-training|one-arm pullup training]], which took years, not months. Ideally the journey doesn't end at "pain-free", it ends at "stronger than before the injury".

## Climbing throughout

**Do not stop climbing.** This was the single most valuable instruction I was given.

- **Warm up thoroughly, every session, no exceptions.** [[warmup-that-made-me-injury-free|Mine is 25–40 minutes]] and doubles as the supplemental strength work above.
- **Climb lots, 2–3 grades below your max.** Easy mileage is the optimal loading; it's also where you can afford to fix the technique habits (locking everything off, feet cutting loose) that kept re-injuring the tendon in the first place.
- **Early on, avoid what provokes it:** deep static lock-offs and big explosive moves are the classic offenders for this injury. Really really avoid sudden footslips which cause the abrupt loading of the tendon. 
- **Reintroduce intensity one notch at a time**, only once Phase 2 is established, with limit bouldering last. The traffic light applies on the wall exactly as it does in the gym.

## The boring substrate

Tendon healing is slow and systemic. Consistent sleep, decent nutrition (avoiding sugar seems to be important for me personally), and actually showing up for the twice-daily sets matter more than any detail of the sets themselves — the [[brachialis-tendon-recovery|video]] covers the habits that dug the hole in the first place. Consistency, progressive overload, adequate recovery. There is no quick fix and no magic; you'll just have to put in the work.
